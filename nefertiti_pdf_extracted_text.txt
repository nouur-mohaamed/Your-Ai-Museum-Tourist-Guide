Nefertiti (circa 1370–1330 BC) was the Great Royal Wife of Pharaoh Akhenaten, ruling during the 18th Dynasty of Ancient Egypt. She played a key role in promoting Atenism, an exclusive sun-disc religion. After Akhenaten’s death, some scholars believe Nefertiti ruled briefly as Neferneferuaten before Tutankhamun ascended. Her reign saw the relocation of the capital from Amarna to Memphis. Nefertiti gained further fame in the 20th century with the discovery of her bust in Berlin's Neues Museum. She held several titles, including "Main King's Wife, his beloved," and her name meant "the beautiful one has come."
Almost nothing is known about Nefertiti's early life. She may have been the daughter of Ay and Tey, or Akhenaten's full sister. Her titles suggest she was not a king's daughter or sister. Nefertiti first appears in reliefs in Thebes around 1353 BCE, accompanying Akhenaten at the Window of Appearance. During the early years of Akhenaten's reign, she held significant political power, appearing more frequently than him in scenes depicting traditional royal roles and smiting enemies. At least six daughters were born to them: Meritaten, Meketaten, Ankhesenpaaten (later Ankhesenamun), Neferneferuaten Tasherit, Neferneferure, and Setepenre. Genetic testing indicates she was not the mother of Tutankhamun.
Amenhotep IV moved the capital to Akhetaten (modern Amarna) in his fourth year, changing his name to Akhenaten and Nefertiti to Neferneferuaten-Nefertiti in year five, marking the rise of the Aten cult. Nefertiti resided in the Great Royal Palace, with Meryre II as her household steward. A tribute ceremony in Year 12 showcased Nefertiti and Akhenaten. Nefertiti likely ruled briefly as Neferneferuaten after Akhenaten's death in Year 17, before being succeeded by Tutankhamun. Discredited theories suggest Nefertiti died or fell out of favor around Year 12.
In 2012, an inscription from Year 16 of Akhenaten's reign was found, confirming Nefertiti's continued role as Great Royal Wife. The inscription, located in Quarry 320, mentions Nefertiti's title and ongoing construction under Akhenaten's authority. Athena van der Perre argues this proves both were alive until nearly the end of Akhenaten's reign. Consequently, the female pharaoh Neferneferuaten likely ruled between Akhenaten's death and Tutankhamun's accession. Neferneferuaten used the title "Effective for her husband," suggesting she was either Nefertiti or Meritaten.
Nefertiti likely ruled as pharaoh Neferneferuaten from Year 5 of Akhenaten's reign until her death during Tutankhamun's Year 3 (1331 BC). Egyptologists like Chris Naunton, Aidan Dodson, Athena van der Perre, Nozomu Kawai, and James Peter Allen support this theory. Allen, in his 2016 paper, suggests Nefertiti acted as a coregent to ensure Tutankhamun's smooth succession. After Akhenaten's death, Nefertiti continued to use the title "Neferneferuaten," indicating her role in religious transitions. Her death marked the end of her influence, leading to Tutankhamun's reign and eventual return to traditional worship.
Aidan Dodson suggests that Nefertiti abandoned Amarna and returned the capital to Memphis and Thebes, while other Egyptologists like Athena van der Perre and Nozomu Kawai argue she ruled independently or solely, possibly due to Tutankhamun's youth. Nefertiti's burial was intended for the Royal Tomb, but she was never interred there. Shabtis and an unfinished tomb (Tomb 29) suggest she may have had a separate tomb planned. The 'Younger Lady' mummy in KV35 was initially proposed as Nefertiti but DNA analysis confirmed it was Tutankhamun's mother, not Nefertiti.
In 2015, archaeologist Nicholas Reeves proposed hidden chambers in Tutankhamun's tomb might contain Nefertiti's remains. Radar scans later refuted this. One of the female mummies in KV21, estimated to be around 45 at death, was suggested as Nefertiti. DNA analysis confirmed her lineage but not her identity. CT-scans indicated a "queenly" pose. She may have been buried with Ankhesenamun, similar to Tiye and the Younger Lady in KV35.

Born circa 1370 BCE, Nefertiti likely died between 1330 and 1331 BCE. Her name means "The beautiful one has come." She took the title "Beautiful is the beauty of Aten, the beautiful one has come" around Akhenaten's 5th regnal year. She was the Great King's Wife, Mistress of Upper and Lower Egypt, and Hereditary Princess. Her parents are believed to be Ay and his second wife Tey, though other theories exist. She was married to Akhenaten.
Akhenaten had six daughters: Meritaten, Meketaten, Ankhesenpaaten (later Ankhesenamun), Neferneferuaten Tasherit, Neferneferure, and Setepenre. Genetic testing confirmed Akhenaten was not Ankhesenpaaten's father. She was prominent in imagery, depicted performing actions typically reserved for male pharaohs. She likely ruled as a sole female pharaoh under the name Ankheperure Neferneferuaten for 2-3 years, aiding in the restoration of traditional religious practices. Her intended burial sites were the Royal Tomb at Akhetaten or Tomb 29, but neither was completed. The KV21B mummy, aged about 45, remains a potential candidate for her remains.
