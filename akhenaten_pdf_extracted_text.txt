Akhenaten, originally Amenhotep IV, ruled from 1353 to 1336 BC or 1351 to 1334 BC, during the Eighteenth Dynasty. He abandoned traditional Egyptian polytheism to introduce Atenism, centered on the sun disk Aten. His religious reforms were reversed after his death. Akhenaten’s monuments and statues were destroyed, and his name was removed from official lists. He was succeeded by Tutankhamun, who restored traditional practices. Akhenaten’s mummy, likely identified as Tutankhamun’s father, was found in 1907. His legacy remains debated, with historians viewing him as revolutionary, idealistic, or heretical.
Akhenaten's youth is uncertain; he may have been born at Amenhotep III's palace or in Memphis. He was tutored by Parennefer. Historical records suggest he may have become High Priest of Ptah in Memphis after his brother's death. Akhenaten married Nefertiti around his fourth year as pharaoh and had several other consorts, including Kiya, who might be Tadukhipa, and foreign princesses. He had seven documented daughters. Akhenaten's son-in-law and likely biological son was Tutankhamun. Genetic testing identified Tutankhamun's parents as the male mummy in KV55 and "The Younger Lady."
Akhenaten's ascension to the throne is debated, with some scholars arguing for a coregency with his father Amenhotep III, lasting up to 12 years, while others propose no coregency or a maximum of two years. In 2014, archaeologists found both kings' names inscribed in the Luxor tomb of Amenhotep-Huy, suggesting a coregency of at least eight years. However, this evidence has been contested by other Egyptologists. Genetic studies indicate that Akhenaten had a son and full sister, but his marriages to his sisters remain controversial, with suggestions of DNA contamination or alternative identities. Theories propose incestuous marriages with daughters to produce heirs, including potential consorts like Meritaten and Meketaten. Smenkhkare’s identity is also debated, with suggestions he could be the same as Neferneferuaten.
Amenhotep IV, who later became Akhenaten, ascended the throne around 1353 or 1351 BC. His early reign maintained traditional Egyptian worship practices, honoring multiple deities like Atum, Osiris, and the Aten. He initially honored Thebes in his titles and continued his father's construction projects there. Artistic representations from this period followed traditional styles. However, Amenhotep IV initiated new constructions dedicated to the Aten in various cities, including Karnak in Thebes, where he planned a large temple complex separate from Amun's structures.
Amenhotep IV, who later became Akhenaten, organized a Sed festival around his second or third regnal year, likely to proclaim his direct rule or honor the Aten. During this festival, he made offerings to the Aten alone. In regnal year five, he changed his name from Amenhotep IV, removing all references to the god Amun, to emphasize his devotion to the Aten. This change is evidenced by the discovery of boundary stelae at Akhetaten bearing his new name.
Egyptologists debate the translation of Akhenaten's name, suggesting "Effective for the Aten" based on his frequent use of the phrase. Some propose his name is misspelled and should be read as Akhenjāti or Aḫanjāti. Akhenaten founded Akhetaten (modern Amarna) on the Nile's east bank, roughly halfway between Thebes and Memphis, in a previously uninhabited area. This site, chosen to belong to no previous deity or ruler, was believed to avoid conflicts with the priesthood of Amun in Thebes. The exact reasons for establishing a new capital remain uncertain but may relate to conflicts with the Amun priesthood.
Akhenaten, during the Eighteenth Dynasty, moved the capital to Akhetaten to break away from the god Amun and his priests. The city featured temples, residences, and government buildings, constructed rapidly using standardized limestone blocks called talatats. By regnal year eight, the royal family moved to Akhetaten, while construction continued elsewhere. Akhenaten faced a complex diplomatic landscape; Egypt's dominance peaked under Thutmose III but faced challenges from the Hittites. The Amarna Letters reveal his foreign policy, including correspondence with various rulers. During his reign, the Hittites expanded, threatening Egypt's vassal states.
As Hittite influence expanded, Egyptian vassals in northern Syria shifted allegiance, leading to regional instability. This prompted Akhenaten to intervene, dispatching police forces (Medjay) to resolve conflicts between Labaya of Shechem and Abdi-Heba of Jerusalem. Akhenaten also sent aid to Rib-Hadda of Byblos, but eventually withdrew support due to Rib-Hadda's frequent pleas, resulting in Rib-Hadda's ouster and execution.

Historians debate Akhenaten's military strategy. Older views suggest he was a pacifist neglecting foreign territories, while modern scholars argue he maintained control over core Egyptian territory through strategic interventions, avoiding costly wars. Known campaigns include a minor Nubian operation and possible Levantine actions against Hittite forces.
Akhenaten's campaigns near Kadesh and in southern Canaan ended in failure, according to scholars like Gabolde, Darnell, and Manassa. These campaigns align with Tutankhamun's Restoration Stela. Akhenaten's final five years (circa 1341-1339 BC) are poorly documented. His last known event was a grand royal reception in year twelve, likely celebrating a jubilee, a marriage, or completing Akhetaten. In 2012, an inscription confirmed Akhenaten and Nefertiti ruling together until year sixteen. Following the reception, a widespread epidemic struck, causing the deaths of several royal family members, including three daughters.
Akhenaten appointed Smenkhkare and possibly Nefertiti as co-regents to ensure the Amarna line's continuity during the epidemic. Smenkhkare ruled briefly, while Nefertiti may have taken the role after him. Akhenaten died in his 17th year, and was initially buried in a tomb east of Akhetaten. His sarcophagus was later destroyed. After Akhenaten's death, the royal court moved back to Thebes, and his body was reburied in KV55.
After Akhenaten's death, Smenkhkare possibly ruled briefly, followed by Nefertiti or Meritaten as Neferneferuaten, who likely reigned for about two years before Tutankhaten ascended, with Ay guiding the administration. The KV55 mummy, identified as Tutankhamun's father in a 2010 study, remains controversial due to genetic anomalies. It could be Akhenaten, Smenkhkare, or another relative. Following Akhenaten's demise, the Aten cult declined; Tutankhamun changed his name and moved away from Akhetaten. Subsequent rulers erased Akhenaten and his family from history, removing their names from monuments.
During Akhenaten's reign, temples were dismantled and stone blocks reused, particularly for Amun temples. Pharaohs like Seti I and Horemheb erased references to Aten-related rulers, altering official king lists. The Amarna period marked a shift from centralized pharaonic religious power to direct piety towards local deities, reducing the pharaoh’s religious authority. This change allowed the Amun priesthood to gain significant political power. Akhenaten's reforms also led to a permanent shift in written language, integrating spoken vernacular into official texts.
In the Druze faith, Akhenaten is recognized as a prophet. Atenism developed from pre-existing solar cults, notably during the Eighteenth Dynasty. Amenhotep III elevated the Aten, adopting the epithet Aten-Tjehen and naming his daughter Beketaten, though he remained devoted to Amun-Ra. Donald B. Redford outlines three stages: early increased depictions, intermediate elevation above other deities, and final strict solar disc representation. Amenhotep IV built open-air Aten temples at Karnak, contrasting the Aten's self-created eternity with traditional gods' decline.
Akhenaten initiated Atenism by changing his name to Akhenaten in Year 5, disbanding traditional priesthoods, and redirecting cult revenues to the Aten. By Year 9, he declared the Aten as the sole universal god, banning other deities and defacing Amun's temples. He also modified the god's title, severing ties with older deities. The Great Hymn to the Aten, found in Ay’s tomb, praises the sun as the sole creator and sustainer of life, emphasizing Akhenaten's role as the divine intermediary.
Akhenaten promoted religious reforms, removing references to other gods and erasing Amun's names. However, evidence suggests his policies were initially tolerant, as seen in the continued use of traditional deities' amulets. After Akhenaten's death, Egypt reverted to polytheism, with Atenism persisting briefly under his successors. The failure of Atenism was attributed to its lack of afterlife beliefs. Tutankhaten and Ankhesenpaaten abandoned Atenism, restoring Amun as the supreme deity, marking the end of Atenism's dominance.
Akhenaten, born Amenhotep IV around 1380/1362 BCE, ascended the throne in 1353 BCE. In 1352 BCE, he declared older deities ineffective, began constructing Aten temples at Thebes, and changed his name to Akhenaten. By 1349 BCE, he officially renamed himself and established the capital Akhetaten. In 1345 BCE, he proclaimed Aten as the sole god, outlawing others. The "Durbar of Year 12" in 1342 BCE showcased foreign tribute. Akhenaten died around 1336/1334 BCE at 38. His successors, including Tutankhamun, Horemheb, and Seti I, reversed his policies, re-establishing traditional gods and temples.
Akhenaten, born Prince Amenhotep (c. 1380 BCE or 1362 BCE), ascended to the throne as Amenhotep IV before changing his name to Akhenaten in his 5th year (c. 1349 BCE). He ruled from approximately 1353 BCE to 1336 BCE. His parents were Pharaoh Amenhotep III and Queen Tiye. Akhenaten married Nefertiti, who held significant religious and political power. He also had secondary wives, including Kiya and Tadukhipa. Akhenaten had several daughters with Nefertiti and a son, Tutankhaten, likely fathered by Kiya or an unnamed sister. He is known for his distinctive elongated physical features and his shift towards monotheism, worshiping Aten.
Akhenaten, the pharaoh who viewed himself as the son and sole earthly intermediary of the sun god Aten, authored or commissioned the "Great Hymn to the Aten," expressing devotion to a single solar deity. He rejected traditional Egyptian religion by banning the cult of Amun and other deities, closing their temples, and redirecting royal patronage to the sun disc. Akhenaten died in his 17th regnal year (c. 1336 BCE / 1334 BCE) and was originally interred in the Royal Tomb at Akhetaten (Amarna). After the city's abandonment, his body was moved to Thebes and identified through genetic testing as the male mummy found in KV55 in the Valley of the Kings.
