Hatshepsut 
Location: The Main Exhibition Galleries and The Grand Staircase 
Hatshepsut was the sixth pharaoh of the Eighteenth Dynasty of Egypt, ruling first as regent and 
then as queen regnant from c. 1479 BC until c. 1458 BC. She was the Great Royal Wife of 
Pharaoh Thutmose II and was Egypt's second confirmed woman who ruled in her own right, 
following Sobekneferu in the Twelfth Dynasty. 
Hatshepsut was born between 1505 and 1495 BC as the eldest daughter of Pharaoh Thutmose I 
and his Great Royal Wife, Ahmose. Around the age of fourteen or fifteen, she was married to her 
half-brother Thutmose II, who was of a similar age. Together, the couple had at least one 
daughter, Princess Neferure, who bore the title of King's Eldest Daughter, suggesting they may 
have had a second daughter who died in childhood. 
Upon the death of Thutmose II, the underage Thutmose III inherited the throne at the age of two. 
Hatshepsut initially served as regent for her stepson from his accession in c. 1479 BC. However, 
by Year 7 of his reign (c. 1472 BC), she assumed the position of pharaoh and adopted the full 
royal titulary, becoming co-ruler alongside Thutmose III. She shared his existing regnal count, 
back-dating her official accession as pharaoh to Year 1. In this coregency, Thutmose III served 
as junior coregent while Hatshepsut held the position of senior coregent. 
Ancient historical accounts state that her rule lasted for about 21 to 22 years, covering both her 
regency and her time as queen regnant. Josephus recorded her reign as lasting 21 years and 9 
months, while Julius Africanus rounded it to 22 years. The earliest archaeological evidence 
acknowledging her status as pharaoh comes from grave goods dated to Year 7. She was 
confirmed as pharaoh by Year 9 during the Punt expedition (c. 1471 BC), and her last dated 
record as pharaoh appears in Year 20 (c. 1460 BC). She no longer appears in contemporary 
records by Year 22 (c. 1458 BC), when Thutmose III undertook his first major foreign campaign, 
signaling the probable year of her death. 
To establish her authority within the patriarchal structure of Ancient Egypt, Hatshepsut adopted 
traditionally male pharaonic roles and regalia. She was depicted wearing the Khat headcloth with 
the uraeus, the shendyt kilt, and the traditional false beard. Her monuments portrayed her with 
masculine physical traits and symbolic regalia, including ram's horns and lion-form sphinxes, to 
present herself as both a mother and father figure to the realm. To reinforce her legitimacy, 
priests promoted a narrative of her divine birth, asserting that she was conceived by her mother 
Ahmose and the god Amun, with the gods Khnum and Heket forming her body and life force. 
She also cited an Oracle of Amun declaring her pharaoh and inscribed claims on her mortuary 
temple that her father, Thutmose I, had appointed her as his designated successor. 
Breaking with the traditional custom of celebrating a royal Heb Sed jubilee after 30 years on the 
throne, Hatshepsut celebrated her first jubilee in Year 15 of her rule as pharaoh. Scholars suggest 
this may have marked 30 years since the death of her father Thutmose I, 30 years since the 
founding of her father's dynasty, or the 30th year of her own life. 
Hatshepsut's reign was characterized by peace, economic prosperity, and extensive trade. She 
restored trade networks disrupted during the Hyksos occupation and funded a major naval 
expedition to the Land of Punt in c. 1471 BC. Her delegation returned with 31 live myrrh trees, 
luxury items, and frankincense, which she ground into kohl eyeliner in the first recorded use of 
the resin. She also sent raiding expeditions to Byblos and the Sinai Peninsula and may have led 
military campaigns into Nubia and Canaan. 
As one of Ancient Egypt's most prolific builders, Hatshepsut commissioned hundreds of 
construction projects across Upper and Lower Egypt, employing the architect Ineni, the royal 
steward Senenmut, and High Priest Hapuseneb. Her major building accomplishments include: 
• Deir el-Bahari: Her mortuary temple complex located opposite Luxor, featuring the 
Djeser-Djeseru (Holy of Holies) and reliefs commemorating her divine birth and the Punt 
expedition. 
• Karnak Temple Complex: Construction of the Red Chapel (Chapelle Rouge) as a 
barque shrine, the restoration of the Precinct of Mut, and the erection of twin obelisks at 
the temple entrance. She ordered two additional obelisks in Year 16; one cracked during 
quarrying and remains at Aswan as the Unfinished Obelisk. 
• Speos Artemidos (Beni Hasan): A rock-cut cavernous temple dedicated to the lioness 
war goddess Pakhet, featuring a famous architrave inscription denouncing the Hyksos. 
Death, Burial, and Mummification of Hatshepsut 
Timeline of Death & Accession 
• Last Known Record as Pharaoh: Year 20, III Peret, Day 2 (c. May 22, 1459 BC). 
• Estimated Date of Death: Year 22, II Peret, Day 10 (c. January 16, 1458 BC or January 
1457 BC), marking the start of Thutmose III's sole reign. 
• Reign Duration: Manetho records her total reign at 21 years and 9 months, consistent 
with her accession date on I Shemu, Day 4 and her death in Year 22. 
Tombs and Interments 
• First Tomb: Construction began while she was Great Royal Wife to Thutmose II, but 
was abandoned as it was undersized for a pharaoh. 
• Valley of the Kings (KV20): 
o Originally quarried for her father, Thutmose I, KV20 was extended with a new 
burial chamber to accommodate a double interment for both Hatshepsut and her 
father. 
o She was initially interred here alongside Thutmose I upon her death. 
• Relocation of Mummies: 
o During the reign of Thutmose III, a new tomb (KV38) was built, and Thutmose I 
was reburied there. 
o Hatshepsut’s mummy may have been relocated around the same time to KV60, 
the tomb of her nurse, Sitre In. 
o These reburials may have been orchestrated by Amenhotep II to reinforce his own 
claim to the throne. 
Funerary Artifacts & Grave Goods 
• KV20 Discoveries: Cleared by Howard Carter in 1903; yielded her quartz sarcophagus. 
• Other Associated Artifacts: 
o A lioness throne (or bedstead). 
o A senet game board with red-jasper, lioness-headed pieces bearing her pharaonic 
title. 
o A signet ring and a partial shabti figurine. 
o A wooden canopic box with an ivory knob bearing her name (found in the DB320 
cache), containing a mummified organ (liver or spleen) and a molar tooth. 
Following her death in Year 22 of Thutmose III's reign, an attempt was made late in his reign 
and into the rule of his son Amenhotep II to erase Hatshepsut from official historical records. 
Her statues were destroyed, her monuments were defaced, and many of her architectural and 
administrative achievements were reassigned to other pharaohs. 
Summary: 
Here is a consolidated list of personal and biographical information about Hatshepsut, 
organized by her lineage, reign, claims to authority, and burial history: 
Birth, Names & Titles 
• Estimated Birth Years: c. 1505–1495 BCE. 
• Estimated Age at Death: Approximately 47 to 50 years old (died c. 1458 BCE). 
• Name Meaning: Ḥꜣt-špswt ("Foremost of Noble Ladies"). 
• Throne Name: Maatkare ("Truth/Order is the Soul of Re"). 
• Extended Name: Khnumetamun Hatshepsut ("Joined with Amun, Foremost of 
Noble Ladies"). 
• Royal & Sacred Titles: 
o King of Upper and Lower Egypt 
o Great Royal Wife 
o God's Wife of Amun 
o Lady of the Two Lands 
o Lord of Khaket 
Family & Lineage 
• Parents: 
o Father: Pharaoh Thutmose I 
o Mother: Great Royal Wife Ahmose 
• Husband: Pharaoh Thutmose II (her half-brother, whom she married around 
age 14 or 15). 
• Children: 
o Princess Neferure: Daughter who held the title "King's Eldest Daughter." 
o Possible Second Daughter: Likely died in childhood. 
• Stepson / Coregent: Pharaoh Thutmose III (son of Thutmose II and secondary 
wife Iset). 
Status, Political Role & Career 
• Historical Precedence: Egypt's second confirmed female ruler in her own right 
(following Sobekneferu of the Twelfth Dynasty), but the first female pharaoh to 
rule during an era of peace and great economic prosperity. 
• Regency to Coregency Transition: 
o Started as regent for the two-year-old Thutmose III in c. 1479 BCE upon 
Thutmose II's death. 
o Assumed full pharaonic power around Year 7 of his reign (c. 1472 BCE), 
crowning herself senior coregent (Osiris) while placing Thutmose III as 
junior coregent (Horus). 
• Pharaonic Regalia & Presentation: Adopted traditional male pharaonic 
symbols—including the Khat headcloth with uraeus, shendyt kilt, and false 
beard—to establish her authority within the Egyptian patriarchy. 
• Divine Legitimacy Claims: 
o Commissioned reliefs portraying a divine birth narrative, claiming the god 
Amun disguised himself as Thutmose I to father her. 
o Inscribed claims on her mortuary temple that Thutmose I had publicly 
designated her as his intended heir. 
• Irregular Jubilee: Celebrated her first Heb Sed jubilee in Year 15 of her reign 
rather than waiting for the traditional 30-year mark. 
• Prolific Builder & Trader: Re-established foreign trade networks (notably the 
expedition to the Land of Punt in c. 1471 BCE) and built major architectural 
works, including her mortuary temple at Deir el-Bahari (Djeser-Djeseru), twin 
obelisks at Karnak, and the rock-cut temple Speos Artemidos. 
Death, Burial & Erasure 
• Date of Death: Year 22, II Peret, Day 10 (c. January 1458 BCE or 1457 BCE), 
after reigning for 21 years and 9 months. 
• Burial Locations: 
o Cliff Tomb: Abandoned during her regency because it was too small for a 
ruling pharaoh. 
o KV20 (Valley of the Kings): Extended her father's tomb to create a 
double interment chamber for both herself and Thutmose I. 
o KV60: Her body was likely relocated here during the reign of Thutmose III 
into the tomb of her nurse, Sitre In. 
• Posthumous Erasure (Damnatio Memoriae): Toward the end of Thutmose III's 
reign and continuing under his son Amenhotep II, an official campaign was 
launched to erase her from history—destroying her statues, chiseling out her 
name from cartouches, and reassigning her monuments to other pharaohs.
